Motion in a straight line 🚀

This topic uses one toolkit: motion in a straight line with constant acceleration. Learn these 4 equations and a few word clues and you can solve almost every question.

🎯 What is this topic, and why does it matter?

Constant acceleration means the speed changes by the same amount every second (a car gaining 2 m/s every second, or gravity pulling things down). That is why one set of equations works.

Projectiles? Anything dropped or thrown straight up or down is this topic, with gravity as the acceleration. Angled projectiles (like a football kicked at an angle) come later, but they reuse the same equations: sideways has no acceleration, up and down has gravity.

Why it’s the base: it is the foundation of Leaving Cert Applied Maths (and physics). It also links to calculus in Higher Level Maths: speed is the rate of change of distance, and acceleration is the rate of change of speed.

🔤 What each letter means

sDisplacement: how far you are from where you started, in a direction (metres, m). Not always the same as distance travelled!

uInitial speed: how fast it’s going at the start of the bit you’re looking at (m/s). “u” comes before “v”.

vFinal speed: how fast it’s going at the end of that bit (m/s).

aAcceleration: how much the speed changes every second (m/s²). Slowing down is a negative a, also called retardation or deceleration.

tTime taken (seconds, s).

💡 Each equation has four of these five letters. Every question gives you three, and you hunt for the one you’re missing.
🧰 The 4 equations (and the missing-letter trick)

Pick the equation by the letter you don’t have and don’t need:

v = u + atno s needed
s = ut + ½at²no v needed
v² = u² + 2asno t needed
s = ½(u + v)tno a needed

Why they work: v = u + at says “new speed = old speed + the speed gained”. The distance one uses the average speed, (u+v)/2, times the time. The others come from swapping one into the other.

🗺️ A 4-step method that always works

1. Draw a quick sketch and pick a positive direction (stick with it!).

2. Write the list s, u, v, a, t and fill in what the question tells you (use the word clues below).

3. Find the equation with the letters you have, plus the one you want.

4. Solve, then ask: does the answer make sense? Include the unit.

🕵️ Word decoder: what the wording secretly tells you
“dropped”, “released”, “starts from rest”, “falls from rest”
u = 0 (it starts still). But v is NOT 0: it speeds up as it falls, so it’s going fast when it lands. Why? You let go, you didn’t throw it.
“comes to rest”, “stops”, “brought to rest”
v = 0 at the end. Why? Rest means speed zero.
“thrown / projected upwards”
u is the throw speed. At the very top the ball stops for an instant, so v = 0 at the maximum height (not at the start, and not at the end!). Why? It has to stop before it can turn around.
“thrown downwards”
u is the throw speed (not 0), and gravity speeds it up. Why? You gave it a push, and gravity adds more.
“uniform / constant speed”
a = 0. Why? Speed isn’t changing. Then simply distance = speed × time.
“retardation”, “decelerates”, “brakes”
a is negative if forwards is positive. Why? It’s slowing down.
“falls”, “under gravity”
Use g = 9.8 m/s². Down is positive if it’s falling; for things thrown up, a = −9.8. Why? Gravity always pulls down, even while the ball is going up (that’s why it slows).
“returns to the starting point”
s = 0 (back where it began).
“in the 7th second”
The distance during that one second only: s₇ − s₆ (distance in 7 s minus distance in 6 s). Why? “In the 7th second” means between 6 and 7 seconds.
“meet”, “collide”, “passes”
Same place, same time: set the two s values equal (mind who started later).
trains/cars “pass each other”
Opposite directions: add the lengths, and the speeds add too (relative speed). Same direction: subtract the speeds.
“average speed”
Total distance ÷ total time. The shortcut (u+v)/2 only works for one stretch of constant acceleration.
🍎 Gravity, up and down (projectiles)

Going up, the ball slows by 9.8 m/s every second. Coming down, it speeds up by 9.8 m/s every second. So:

⬆️ Time up = time down (it’s symmetrical).
⏸️ At the top: v = 0.
🔁 It lands back at the start with the same speed it left with, just pointing down.
⏱️ Time to the top: t = u ÷ 9.8. Max height: h = u² ÷ 19.6.

⚠️ “Between two instants it is 39.2 m high” (a ball going up and coming down): you get two times, once on the way up, once on the way down. The answer is the difference between them.
⚠️ Traps that catch people
Distance ≠ displacement. A ball thrown up and back down travels 2× the height as distance, but its displacement is smaller. Read whether it asks for “distance travelled” or “how far from the start”.
Units. Check minutes → seconds (2 minutes = 120 s) and km/h → m/s (divide by 3.6).
Quadratics give two answers. A negative time (or one that doesn’t fit the story) is thrown away.
Signs. Pick up or down as positive and stay consistent; mixing them is the number one cause of wrong answers.
Don’t assume v = 0. Only use it for “comes to rest”, “stops” or “at the top”.
Two stages? The final speed of the first stage is the initial speed (u) of the next.
📈 Speed–time graphs

Speed on the up axis, time along the bottom.

📐 Gradient (slope) = acceleration. Steeper means a bigger a. Flat means a = 0 (constant speed). Sloping down means slowing.

🟦 Area under the graph = distance. A triangle is ½ × base × height, and a trapezium is the average of the parallel sides × width.

Questions like “accelerates, cruises, then brakes” are a trapezium: ramp up, flat, ramp down.

🧪 Two worked examples

Thrown up: A ball is thrown up at 39.2 m/s. How long to reach the top?
u = 39.2, v = 0 (top!), a = −9.8, t = ? → v = u + at → 0 = 39.2 − 9.8t → t = 4 s ✅

Dropped: A ball is dropped from 78.4 m. How long to land?
s = 78.4, u = 0 (dropped), a = 9.8, t = ? → s = ut + ½at² → 78.4 = 4.9t² → t = 4 s ✅
Its landing speed: v = 0 + 9.8 × 4 = 39.2 m/s, so v was definitely not 0!

Tip: use g = 9.8 m/s² in every motion question.

Binomials & Pascal’s triangle 🔺

A binomial is an expression with two terms, like (x + y). To expand (x + y)⁵ you could multiply five brackets together, but there’s a much faster pattern.

🔺 Pascal’s triangle (the coefficient cheat-sheet)

Start with 1s down both edges. Every other number is the sum of the two numbers above it. Row n gives the coefficients of (x + y)ⁿ.

1power 01 1power 11 2 1power 21 3 3 1power 31 4 6 4 1power 41 5 10 10 5 1power 5

Why it works: each term in the next row is built from the two terms above it when you multiply by one more bracket, so the numbers add.

🧭 The pattern in every expansion

In (x + y)ⁿ: the power of x goes down from n to 0, the power of y goes up from 0 to n, and the powers in each term always add to n. There are n + 1 terms.

Example: (x + y)³ = 1x³ + 3x²y + 3xy² + 1y³

🧮 The general term (for big powers)

The term with b to the power r in (a + b)ⁿ is: ⁿCr × aⁿ⁻ʳ × bʳ.

Why: ⁿCr counts the ways to choose which r of the n brackets give you a b (the rest give a). Use the nCr button on your calculator.

💡 Treat the whole bracket term as one block: in (2x − 3)⁴ the “a” is 2x and the “b” is −3.
✍️ Worked example: expand (2x − 3)⁴

Row 4 is 1, 4, 6, 4, 1. Powers of 2x go down, powers of (−3) go up:

1(2x)⁴ + 4(2x)³(−3) + 6(2x)²(−3)² + 4(2x)(−3)³ + 1(−3)⁴
= 16x⁴ − 96x³ + 216x² − 216x + 81

Notice the signs alternate because b is negative. Odd powers of a negative stay negative, even powers turn positive.

🎯 Finding one term: “coefficient of xᵏ”

Coefficient of x⁵ in (x − 2)⁸: the power of x is 8 − r, so 8 − r = 5 → r = 3. Term = ⁸C₃ × x⁵ × (−2)³ = 56 × (−8) = −448.

Term independent of x in (x − 1/x)¹²: “independent of x” means the power of x is 0. Each term has x to the (12 − 2r), so 12 − 2r = 0 → r = 6, and the term is ¹²C₆ = 924 (the signs cancel because (−1)⁶ = 1).

Why 12 − 2r? x¹²⁻ʳ × (1/x)ʳ = x¹²⁻ʳ × x⁻ʳ.

⚠️ Traps that catch people
Raise the whole term: (2x)³ = 8x³, not 2x³.
Watch negatives: (−3)² = +9 but (−3)³ = −27.
“Coefficient” means just the number part, including its sign.
n + 1 terms, not n.
1/x is x⁻¹. Use that to combine powers.
⌨️ How to type your answer

Use ^ for powers (x^3) and / for fractions (12/x). For “expand” questions, write it out without brackets. Terms can be in any order.

Rearranging & formulae 🔁

Changing the subject means getting one letter on its own. The golden rule: whatever you do to one side, do to the other.

🧅 Peel it like an onion

Undo operations in reverse BIDMAS order: undo adding/subtracting first, then multiplying/dividing, then powers. Make y = 3x − 5 into x = (y + 5)/3.

Why: the letter you want is wrapped in layers. You unwrap from the outside in.

🍝 Fractions: clear them first

To get rid of fractions, multiply EVERYTHING on both sides by the common denominator. To add fractions, put them over a common denominator.

1/u + 1/v = 1/f → (v + u)/uv = 1/f → flip both sides → f = uv/(u + v).

Only cancel factors (things multiplied), never terms (things added). You can cancel the 2s in (2x)/2 but not in (2 + x)/2.
🔁 The letter appears twice

Given x = (y² − xz)/(y + z), find z:

1. Clear the fraction: x(y + z) = y² − xz
2. Expand: xy + xz = y² − xz
3. Get all the z terms on one side: xy + 2xz = y²... or 2xz = y² − xy
4. Divide: z = (y² − xy)/(2x)

Why 2xz? xz appears on both sides, so moving one over gives two of them. When a letter appears twice, gather them, factor out the letter, then divide.

📐 Using formulae (modelling)

Rectangle, perimeter 30: 2l + 2w = 30 → w = 15 − l → area A = l(15 − l).

Closed cylinder, surface area 28: 2πr² + 2πrh = 28 → h = (14 − πr²)/(πr). Then V = πr²h = r(14 − πr²) = 14r − πr³.

Cosine rule: a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A → 2bc cos A = b² + c² − a² → cos A = (b² + c² − a²)/(2bc).

Why this works: the given fact (the perimeter, the surface area) lets you swap out one unknown so the answer is in terms of just one letter.

🧠 Identity or equation?

An identity is true for all values of x (you may be asked to prove it). An equation is true for some values (you solve it).

⚠️ Traps
Change the sign when a term crosses the =.
Multiply or divide every term, not just one.
Don’t flip only one side of a fraction equation. Combine into one fraction per side first.
⌨️ How to type your answer

Type it like (y^2-x*y)/(2*x) or l(15-l). Use pi or π for π, and always put brackets around a whole denominator. Any equivalent form is accepted, and “z =” at the start is optional.

Factorising 🧩

Factorising means writing an expression as brackets multiplied together (the opposite of expanding). Answers need brackets to count as fully correct.

🗂️ Order of attack

1. Common factor first (pull it out). 2. Two terms with squares → difference of two squares. 3. Three terms → trinomial. 4. Cubes → sum/difference of cubes.

Always check by expanding your brackets back out.

⚔️ Difference of two squares
a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)

Remember as (First)² − (Second)² = (First − Second)(First + Second). Example: 400x² − 49y² = (20x)² − (7y)² = (20x − 7y)(20x + 7y).

Why no middle term? Expand it: the −ab and +ab cancel. It only works with a minus between two squares (a sum of squares won’t factorise).

It works with brackets too: (x + y)² − z² = (x + y − z)(x + y + z).

🔧 Trinomials (the AC method)

For ax² + bx + c: multiply a × c, find two numbers that multiply to ac and add to b, split the middle term, then group.

2x² − 5x − 12: a×c = −24. Numbers: −8 and +3 (they multiply to −24, add to −5). 2x² − 8x + 3x − 12 = 2x(x − 4) + 3(x − 4) = (2x + 3)(x − 4).

Sign clues: if c is positive, both numbers have the same sign (as b). If c is negative, they have different signs.

With two letters (x² + 5xy − 14y²) it works the same, and each bracket keeps both letters: (x + 7y)(x − 2y).

🧊 Sum and difference of cubes
a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²)
a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²)

Sign trick (SOAP): the first bracket has the Same sign, then Opposite, then Always Positive.

Example: 8x³ − 27 = (2x)³ − 3³ = (2x − 3)(4x² + 6x + 9).

🔍 Perfect squares

If the first and last terms are squares and the middle is twice their product: a² + 2ab + b² = (a + b)². Example: 4p² − 4p + 1 = (2p − 1)².

⚠️ Traps
Treat a bracket like one letter: (a − 1)² + 2(a − 1) − 15, let u = a − 1, factorise u² + 2u − 15 = (u + 5)(u − 3), then put it back: (a + 4)(a − 4).
Pull out the common factor before anything else.
Check the signs by expanding.
⌨️ How to type your answer

Type it like (2x+3)(x-4) or (bx+c)^2. Use ^ for powers. The order of brackets and of terms doesn’t matter.
